UNIT 1 · LECTURE 1.1

Translational Kinematics

Describing motion in one and two dimensions: position, velocity, and acceleration, with no regard for what causes the motion. Sign convention: right and up are positive; left and down are negative.

Key terms

Displacement \(\Delta x\)
Vector. Straight-line change in position, \(x_f - x_i\). Needs an origin and a positive direction. Can be negative. Ignores the path.
Distance \(d\)
Scalar. Total length of the path actually traveled. Never negative.
Average velocity \(v_{av}\)
Net displacement ÷ time. Vector. Zero for a round trip.
Average speed
Total distance ÷ time. Scalar. Never negative.
Instantaneous velocity
Velocity at one instant: \(\Delta t \to 0\). Equal to the slope of the tangent line on an x–t graph.
Uniform velocity
Equal displacements in equal time intervals. The x–t graph is a straight line.
Acceleration \(a\)
Rate of change of velocity (m/s²). Vector. Slope of the v–t graph. Slope of the tangent gives instantaneous acceleration.
Free fall
Motion under gravity only: \(a = -g = -9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\) (up is +). A ball thrown upward is also in free fall.
Projectile motion
2D motion with \(a_x = 0\) and \(a_y = -g\). The path is a parabola. The x and y motions are independent but share the same time \(t\).
Range \(R\) / max height \(H\)
Horizontal distance traveled (same launch and landing height) / peak height, where \(v_y = 0\) but \(v_x \neq 0\).

Formula sheet

Average velocity & speed

\[ v_{av}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}=\frac{x_f-x_i}{t_f-t_i}\qquad \text{speed}_{av}=\frac{d_{total}}{\Delta t} \]

"Endpoints" keyword: only the initial and final points matter for velocity.

Instantaneous velocity & acceleration

\[ v=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}=\frac{dx}{dt}\qquad a=\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} \]

"Instant" keyword: slope of the tangent line.

Average acceleration

\[ a_{av}=\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}=\frac{v_f-v_i}{t_f-t_i} \]

Slope of the line joining two points on a v–t graph.

Eq. 1 · velocity–time

\[ v_2=v_1+at \]

No displacement involved. Constant \(a\) only.

Eq. 2 · position–time

\[ x_2=x_1+v_1t+\tfrac12at^2 \]

No final velocity involved.

Eq. 3 · definition of a

\[ a=\frac{v_2-v_1}{t_2-t_1} \]

Same idea as Eq. 1, rearranged.

Eq. 4 · "timeless" equation

\[ v_2^2=v_1^2+2a(x_2-x_1) \]

Use this when time isn't given or asked for.

Handy extra (not on slides)

\[ x_2-x_1=\tfrac12(v_1+v_2)\,t \]

No acceleration needed. Comes from Eq. 1 + Eq. 2.

Free fall (replace x→y, a→−g)

\[ v_2=v_1-gt \qquad y_2=y_1+v_1t-\tfrac12gt^2 \]\[ v_2^2=v_1^2-2g(y_2-y_1)\qquad g=9.81\ \text{m/s}^2 \]

Projectile: components

\[ v_{1x}=v_1\cos\theta\qquad v_{1y}=v_1\sin\theta \]\[ x_2=x_1+v_{1x}t \quad (v_x \text{ constant}) \]\[ v_{2y}=v_{1y}-gt\qquad y_2=y_1+v_{1y}t-\tfrac12gt^2 \]

Range and max height

\[ R=\frac{v_1^2\sin 2\theta}{g}\qquad H=\frac{v_1^2\sin^2\theta}{2g} \]

R only works when launch height = landing height. Max range occurs at θ = 45°.

Non-constant acceleration

\[ \Delta x=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}v\,dt\qquad \Delta v=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}a\,dt \]

Eqs. 1–4 do not apply here.

Reading graphs

GraphSlope meansClues
x vs tvelocitySteeper means faster. Negative slope means moving left. Curving up means speeding up.

How to solve from the slides

  1. Understand the problem. List every given quantity and the unknown.
  2. Draw a schematic. Mark position 1 (start) and position 2 (where the unknown is) on an axis, each with x (or y), t, and v.
  3. Set defaults. Unspecified start: \(x_1=0\), \(t_1=0\). Ground: \(y=0\). "At rest" or "stops" means \(v=0\) at that point.
  4. Assign signs. Right and up are +. Gravity: \(a=-9.81\) m/s².
  5. Pick the equation that contains your unknown and no other unknowns (see the table above). Solve, then check that the units and sign make sense.
  6. Projectiles: split into x (constant velocity) and y (free fall). Time is the bridge: often you solve one direction for \(t\), then use \(t\) in the other.

You jog around the Intramuros grounds starting from Lyceum. Total distance is 2.20 km. After 25.0 min you're back at Lyceum. Find your average speed and average velocity (m/s).

Given\(d=2.20\ \text{km}=2200\ \text{m}\), \(\Delta t=25.0\ \text{min}=1500\ \text{s}\), start = end
Findaverage speed, \(v_{av}\)
  1. Convert units: \(2.20\ \text{km}\times1000=2200\ \text{m}\), and \(25.0\ \text{min}\times60=1500\ \text{s}\).

slides 47–48

You slide your book to a classmate 0.50 m away. The table slows it at −1.00 m/s². With what initial velocity should you push it so it stops right in front of your classmate?

Falling projector

slides 53–55

A projector falls from rest from 2.5 m above the floor. What is its speed just before hitting the floor?

Given\(y_1=2.5\ \text{m},\ y_2=0,\ v_1=0,\ a=-g=-9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\)
Find\(v_2\) (speed)
  1. No time is given, so use Eq. 4 with \(a=-g\):
    \[ v_2^2=v_1^2+2a(y_2-y_1)=0+2(-9.81)(0-2.5)=49.05 \]

An object starts from rest at the origin with constant acceleration 4 m/s² along x. Its average velocity as it goes from x = 2 m to x = 8 m is:

Velocity of a projectile

The velocity of a projectile equals its initial velocity plus ______.

Stone off a cliff

slides 83–84 · MCQ

A stone is thrown from the top of a 59.4 m cliff with an upward velocity component of 19.5 m/s. How long is it in the air?

  • A. 4.0
  • B. 5.0
  • C. 6.0
  • D. 7.0
  • E. 8.0 s

Graph A rises then falls. Graph B is a straight rising line. Graph C rises steeply then flattens. Graph D starts flat then curves sharply upward. Which object (a) moves with constant speed, (b) reverses direction, (c) starts slowly then speeds up?

Concept checks slide 30

Can you have zero instantaneous velocity but nonzero average velocity?

Yes. The object stopped for a moment, then kept going.

Zero average velocity but nonzero instantaneous velocity?

Yes. It moved, then returned to its starting point.

Negative net displacement and zero average velocity?

No. Average velocity = net displacement ÷ time, so a nonzero displacement gives a nonzero average velocity.

Negative net displacement and positive instantaneous velocity?

Yes. It moved right at one instant, but ended up left of where it started.

Is the velocity zero at a projectile's maximum height?

No. Only \(v_y=0\). The horizontal velocity \(v_x\) is still there.

UNIT 2 · LECTURE 1.2

Newton's Laws of Motion

Forces explain why motion changes. Find the net force with a free-body diagram (FBD), then use \(\sum\vec F=m\vec a\). These slides use \(g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Key terms

Force \(\vec F\)
A vector push or pull that makes a mass accelerate. Unit: newton, 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².
Net force \(\sum\vec F\)
The vector sum of all forces acting on the object. Add them by components.
Normal force \(N\)
Contact force from a surface, always perpendicular to it. It exists only when there is contact.
Friction \(f\)
Opposes motion along the surface. \(f=\mu N\). Static (\(\mu_s\)): about to move. Kinetic (\(\mu_k\)): already sliding.
Tension \(T\)
Pull along a string or rope, directed away from the object. Same throughout a massless rope.
Weight \(W\)
Gravitational force \(W=mg\), always downward. Measured in newtons.
Mass \(m\)
Intrinsic amount of matter (kg). Not the same as weight.
Inertia
An object's tendency to stay at rest or keep moving at constant velocity.
Equilibrium
\(\sum\vec F=0\), so \(a=0\). The object is at rest or moving at constant velocity.
Free-body diagram
The object drawn as a point with arrows for forces acting on it only. Velocity and acceleration are not forces, so leave them out.
Action–reaction pair
Equal and opposite forces that act on different bodies, so they never cancel each other.
Contact vs. non-contact
Contact: normal, friction, tension, spring. Non-contact (long-range): gravity, magnetic.

Formula sheet

1st law · Inertia

\[ \sum\vec F=0\iff \vec a=0 \]\[ \sum F_x=0,\quad \sum F_y=0 \]

Holds for objects at rest or at constant velocity.

2nd law · Acceleration

\[ \sum\vec F=m\vec a \]\[ \sum F_x=ma_x,\quad \sum F_y=ma_y \]

The x and y components are independent.

3rd law · Interaction

\[ \vec F_{A\text{ on }B}=-\vec F_{B\text{ on }A} \]

Magnitude & direction

\[ F=\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2}\qquad \theta=\tan^{-1}\!\frac{F_y}{F_x} \]

θ is measured from +x. Check the quadrant from the signs of \(F_x\) and \(F_y\). The same formulas give the size and direction of \(a\).

Weight & friction

\[ W=mg\qquad f_s\le\mu_sN\qquad f_k=\mu_kN \]

Inclined plane (angle θ)

\[ W_\parallel=mg\sin\theta\qquad W_\perp=mg\cos\theta \]\[ N=mg\cos\theta\qquad f=\mu mg\cos\theta \]

Tilt your axes: x along the incline, y perpendicular to it. Frictionless: \(a=g\sin\theta\).

Elevator / hanging mass

\[ T=m(g+a)\ \text{(accelerating up)} \]\[ T=m(g-a)\ \text{(accelerating down)} \]

Atwood machine (2 hanging masses)

\[ a=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{m_1+m_2}\qquad T=\frac{2m_1m_2g}{m_1+m_2} \]

Block on table pulled by hanging mass

\[ a=\frac{m_hg-\mu_km_tg}{m_h+m_t} \]

Set \(\mu_k=0\) for a frictionless table.

How to solve FBD method

  1. Pick the object and treat it as a point.
  2. Draw axes. On an incline, put x along the slope.
  3. Draw only forces ON the object: weight (down), normal (⊥ surface), friction (opposite to motion), tension (along the rope).
  4. Resolve each force into x and y components.
  5. Check the state of motion. At rest or constant velocity: \(a=0\) on that axis. No motion along an axis: \(a=0\) there.
  6. Write \(\sum F_x=ma_x\) and \(\sum F_y=ma_y\) and solve. Use kinematics (Unit 1) if distance or time is needed.
  7. Several bodies? Draw a separate FBD for each. They share \(a\) and \(T\) if joined by a taut rope.

Jacket on a clothesline

slides 65–67

A 2.00 kg jacket hangs at the middle of a clothesline. Each side makes 22° with the horizontal. Find the tension in each side.

N5

Brakes!

slide 71

Tire–road friction: μₖ = 0.61, μₛ = 0.89. A car moving at 24 m/s brakes. Find (a) the minimum stopping distance and (b) the stopping distance with the wheels locked (skidding).

  1. On a level road, \(N=mg\). Friction is the only horizontal force: \(-\mu mg=ma\Rightarrow a=-\mu g\). The mass cancels.
  2. Kinematics with \(v_2=0\): \(0=v_1^2-2\mu g\,d\Rightarrow d=\dfrac{v_1^2}{2\mu g}\).
  3. (a) The shortest stop happens when the tires roll without skidding, which gives maximum static friction:
    \[ d=\frac{24^2}{2(0.89)(9.80)}=\frac{576}{17.44}=33\ \text{m} \]
  4. (b) Skidding means kinetic friction:
    \[ d=\frac{576}{2(0.61)(9.80)}=48\ \text{m} \]

Answer(a) ≈ 33 m · (b) ≈ 48 m. This is why anti-lock brakes help.

N6

Jackie Chan on the ice cliff

slide 72

A 70 kg man dangles over a cliff edge, roped to a 940 kg rock 51 m from the edge on flat ice. How long until the rock goes over if (a) the ice is frictionless, (b) μₖ = 0.25?

  1. Two FBDs with the same \(a\) and \(T\). Rock: \(T=Ma\). Man: \(mg-T=ma\).
  2. (a) Add the equations:
    \[ a=\frac{mg}{M+m}=\frac{70(9.80)}{940+70}=\frac{686}{1010}=0.679\ \text{m/s}^2 \]
  3. The rock starts from rest and must cover 51 m: \(51=\frac12(0.679)t^2\Rightarrow t=\sqrt{\frac{102}{0.679}}=12.3\ \text{s}\).
  4. (b) Friction on the rock: \(f=\mu_kMg=0.25(940)(9.80)=2303\ \text{N}\). The man's weight is only 686 N, so the net force can't move the rock: \(a=\frac{686-2303}{1010}<0\).

Answer(a) ≈ 12 s · (b) the rock never slides, so he's safe.

Concept checks slides 18–19, 43

True or false: for a body to be in equilibrium, no force should act on it.

False. It needs zero net force. Many forces can act if they cancel.

True or false: if a body is moving, it can't be in equilibrium.

False. Constant velocity (a = 0) is equilibrium.

An elephant and a feather fall with no air resistance. Same acceleration? Same force?

Same acceleration (g), different force. W = mg, so the elephant's weight is far larger.

With air resistance, how can a feather fall at constant velocity?

Drag grows until it equals the weight. Then the net force is zero (1st law), so it falls at terminal velocity.

Why don't action–reaction forces cancel?

They act on different objects. Only forces on the same object add up to the net force.

UNIT 3 · LECTURE 2.1

Work, Energy & Power

Energy is a scalar, so it's often easier to use than vector forces. Work moves energy in or out, and conservation tracks it. These slides use \(g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Key terms

Energy
Scalar, no exact definition. Takes many forms: potential, kinetic, thermal, radiant. Unit: joule (J).
Kinetic energy \(K\)
Energy of motion: \(K=\frac12mv^2\). Never negative.
Gravitational PE \(U_G\)
Stored energy of position: \(U_G=mgh\). h is measured from a chosen reference, usually the ground.
Elastic PE \(U_E\)
Stored in a stretched or compressed spring: \(U_E=\frac12kx^2\). x is measured from the natural length.
Work \(W\)
Energy transferred by a force over a displacement: \(W=Fd\cos\theta\). Scalar. 1 J = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m²/s².
Positive / negative / zero work
θ = 0° gives +. θ = 180° gives − (friction). θ = 90° gives 0 (normal force, or carrying a bag horizontally).
Work–energy theorem
Total work on an object = change in its kinetic energy.
Hooke's law
Spring restoring force \(F=-kx\). It's negative because it points back toward the natural length. k = spring constant (N/m).
Conservative force
Work depends only on the start and end points, not the path. Zero work around a closed loop. Examples: gravity, springs.
Non-conservative force
Work depends on the path, and mechanical energy is lost (to heat). Examples: friction, drag.
Mechanical energy \(E\)
\(E=K+U_G+U_E\). Conserved when only conservative forces do work.
Power \(P\)
Rate of doing work. Watt: 1 W = 1 J/s. 1 hp = 746 W. A kWh is energy, not power.
Stable equilibrium
Objects tend toward a position of minimum U. Force points toward lower potential energy.

Formula sheet

Work by a constant force

\[ W=Fd\cos\theta=\vec F\cdot\vec d \]\[ \vec F\cdot\vec d=F_xx+F_yy+F_zz \]

θ is the angle between the force and the displacement.

Total work (several forces)

\[ W_{tot}=W_1+W_2+\dots=F_{net}\,d\cos\theta_{net} \]

Work by friction

\[ \text{level: } W_f=-\mu mgd \]\[ \text{incline: } W_f=-\mu mgd\cos\theta \]

Work by a varying force

\[ W=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}F(x)\,dx=\text{area under } F\text{–}x \]

Kinetic energy & work–energy theorem

\[ K=\tfrac12mv^2\qquad W_{tot}=K_2-K_1 \]

Always valid, whatever forces are acting.

Potential energies

\[ U_G=mgh\qquad U_E=\tfrac12kx^2 \]

Springs

\[ F=-kx \]\[ W_{\text{by spring}}=-\left(\tfrac12kx_2^2-\tfrac12kx_1^2\right) \]

The work you do to stretch a spring from 0 to x is \(+\frac12kx^2\).

Conservative work

\[ W_{cons}=-\Delta U=-(U_2-U_1) \]

Conservation of mechanical energy

\[ K_1+U_{G1}+U_{E1}=K_2+U_{G2}+U_{E2} \]

Use this when only gravity and springs do work.

With friction / other forces

\[ K_1+U_1+W_{other}=K_2+U_2 \]\[ \Delta E_{mech}=W_{other} \]

\(W_{other}\) is negative for friction.

Power

\[ P_{av}=\frac{W}{\Delta t}=Fv \]\[ 1\ \text{W}=1\ \text{J/s}\quad 1\ \text{hp}=746\ \text{W} \]

Force from potential energy

\[ F_x=-\frac{dU}{dx} \]

Check: \(U=mgy\) gives \(F=-mg\). \(U=\frac12kx^2\) gives \(F=-kx\).

How to solve energy method

  1. Pick position 1 and position 2. Position 2 is where the unknown is.
  2. Set the reference height (h = 0), usually the ground or the lowest point.
  3. List v, h, and spring x at each position. "Rest", "stops", or "max height" means v = 0. A spring that's not touching or at natural length means x = 0.
  4. Cross out zero terms: K = 0 if not moving, U_G = 0 at the reference, U_E = 0 with no spring compression, W_other = 0 if there's no friction.
  5. Check for friction. If there is any, add \(W_{other}=-fd\).
  6. Write \(K_1+U_1+W_{other}=K_2+U_2\) and solve.

An object leaves the ground and returns to the same spot. Is the work done by gravity zero?

True. \(\Delta y=0\), so \(W_g=-mg\Delta y=0\).

When it returns to its starting spot, is its gravitational PE zero?

Not necessarily. U = mgy depends on where you put y = 0.

At the top of a swing, K = 0. Where did the energy go?

It's stored as potential energy and turns back into kinetic energy on the way down.

Name two conservative and two non-conservative forces.

Conservative: gravity, spring force. Non-conservative: friction, air drag.

A kilowatt-hour measures what?

Energy, not power. 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
